An Intoduction to Geometric Algebra

This page is still WIP, some things may be incomplete

This introduction uses the plane-based interpretation of geometric algebra, even in VGA, because it works alot better for using PGA and CGA without having to create dual versions of all the operators and products
And because its geometrically more intuitive, even if vectors arent arrows like people from linear algebra might be used to

VGA

3D VGA (vector/vanilla geometric algebra) has 3 basis elements $e_1$, $e_2$, and $e_3$, which correspond to the $x$, $y$, and $z$ axis planes respectively that all pass through the origin
All the basis elements square to 1:
$ (e_1)^2 = 1 $
$ (e_2)^2 = 1 $
$ (e_3)^2 = 1 $
(This is similar to how $i$ in complex numbers is a magic symbol that squares to -1)

The basis elements are also anti-commutative, where reversing their multiplication order introduces a minus sign: $ e_i e_j = -e_j e_i $ when $ (i \not= j) $
And because dealing with basis elements that are multiplied together is so common there is a shorthand $ e_{ij} = e_i e_j $

All values in geometric algebra are both objects and transformations
Multiplying values together like $AB$ gives you a transformation that does $A$ and then $B$
Multiplying any value by a non-zero scalar does not change the object or transformation it represents, but it will multiply its weight
Adding values together like $A + B$ gives you a weighted average of both $A$ and $B$, this may not always give an object that has a geometric meaning, but the transformation will be some average of the two
E.g. if you do $2A + B$ then the resulting transformation will be more like $A$, compared to if you did just $A + B$

Orientations

$e_1 + e_2$ is the plane that is at a 45 degree angle half way between $e_1$ and $e_2$, but $e_1 + 2e_2$ is at a 60 degree angle, more like $e_2$ than $e_1$
Which way is "more like $e_2$" depends on arbitrary normal directions that is chosen for each basis plane, it will be rotating the way that makes the normal of the resulting plane more like $e_2$
It does not matter which normal direction is chosen for the basis planes, as long as it is chosen consistiently
The plane $-p$ has its normal going in the opposite direction to the plane $p$

Operators

Grade Selection

The grade of a term is how many basis elements are multiplied together, scalars like $1$ are grade 0, vectors like $e_1$ are grade 1, bivectors like $e_{12}$ are grade 2, etc
$ \langle A \rangle _i $ selects the $i$th grade parts of $A$
If there is no subscript like $\langle A \rangle$ then it gets the scalar part (grade 0) of $A$
E.g. $ \langle 1 + 2e_1 + 3e_2 + 4e_{12} + 5e_{13} \rangle _1 = 2e_1 + 3e_2 $ because $e_1$ and $e_2$ have grade 1

Reverse

The reverse operator ($\dagger$) gives the reverse of any transformation
It gives the same multivector but with all the basis elements of its terms reversed
E.g. $ (e_{123})^\dagger = e_{321} $ or $ (e_{12})^\dagger = e_{21} $
For scalars, this does nothing

Sandwich Product

This isnt an operator but its how transformations are applied to values
$ A^\dagger B A $ applies the transformation $A$ to the object $B$
This works because it gives the transformation that first does the reverse of $A$, then the transformation $B$, and then applies the transformation $A$ to go undo the reverse $A$
Its similar to how rotating vectors around points works in linear algebra, people commonly do $rotate(B - A) + A$ to rotate the vector $B$ around the point $A$
Note: The sandwich product will not return the same kind of object as $B$ unless $A$ is an all-even or all-odd grade multivector

Wedge Product

The wedge product ($\wedge$) gets the intersection of objects, its definition is $ A_i \wedge B_j = \langle A_iB_j \rangle _{i + j} $
where $A_i$ has grade $i$, and $B_j$ has grade $j$
The wedge product is anti-commutative: $ A \wedge B = -B \wedge A $
For general multivectors, distribute through linearity to all combinations of terms of the 2 multivectors
The wedge product for linearly independant values is the same as the geometric product, e.g. $e_1 \wedge e_2 = e_{12}$

Vectors are planes and they do planar reflections, if you intersect 2 planes then you get a line (a bivector) which does line reflections, if you intersect a plane and a line, then you get a point (a trivector) which does point reflections

Dual

Note: The dual is hard to define formally or as a single equation, so thats why this definition relies on examples and less techincal language
The dual ($\star$) is a unary operator that is defined as the thing that you would multiply a term by to get the "pseudoscalar" (usually called $I$, its the highest grade object in the algebra being used)
Ignoring scaling, its the solution to the equation $ A (\star A) = I $ for a single term
To get the dual of a multivector, distribute through linearity and do the dual operation on every term in the multivector
It mostly exists to define the regressive product
In 3D VGA $ I = e_{123} $ and some examples are:
$ \star 1 = e_{123} $
$ \star e_1 = e_{23} $
$ \star e_2 = -e_{13} $ (the negative sign is because reordering the basis elements to produce the $I$ negates the result: $ e_2(-e_{13}) = -e_{213} = e_{123} $)
$ \star e_{12} = e_3 $
$ \star 5e_1 = 5e_{23} $ (any scaling of the input is conserved in the output)

Regressive Product

The regressive product ($\vee$) joins objects together, its definition is $ A \vee B = \star (\star A \wedge \star B) $
Like the wedge product, the regressive product is anti-commutative: $ A \vee B = -B \vee A $

If you join 2 points you get the line connecting them, and if you join a point and a line then you get the plane that passes through the line and the point
The regressive product is mostly useless in VGA, but it becomes more useful in other algebras like PGA

Projection

Not an operator, and not useful until PGA, but the formula for projecting any object $A$ onto any object $B$ is $(A \cdot B)B$

PGA

PGA (projective geometric algebra) is just like VGA, except it adds a new basis element $e_0$ that represents the plane at infinity
$e_0$ is different from the other basis elements because $ (e_0)^2 = 0 $
Note: Sometimes people will use $e_ \infty$ instead of $e_0$ since its the plane at infinity
Fun Fact: If you make $e_0$ square to 1 instead, then you get Spherical PGA, and if you make $e_0$ square to -1, then you get Hyperbolic PGA, so PGA can even do non-euclidean geometry!
$e_0$ is what allows planes to move away from the origin
Because adding objects in geometric algebra is like an average, $e_1 + e_0$ is $e1$ but "more like $e_0$" which moves $e1$ in its normal direction that was chosen earlier, specifically it is 1 unit from the origin
Just like all addition, the relative weight of the things that you are adding matters, $e_1 + 2e_0$ moves $e_1$ 2 units in its normal direction
And now, you can intersect planes that are not at the origin to get lines that are not at the origin, and then intersect those to get points which arent at the origin too!
PGA has the ability to do any rigid transformation

CGA

CGA (conformal geometric algebra) is like PGA, except $e_0$ is split into $e_+$ and $e_-$
$ (e_+)^2 = 1 $
$ (e_-)^2 = -1 $
For convenience, $e_0$ can then be defined as $e_0 = e_+ + e_-$, so that equations can be written as if you were using PGA
Note: Sometimes people will use $e_ \infty$ or $n_ \infty$ instead of $e_0$ when using CGA, since its the sphere at infinity, and then use $n_0 = e_+ - e_-$ to refer to the zero-radius sphere at the origin
